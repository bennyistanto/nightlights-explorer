Mudik 2019: looking for an exodus in the dark

Every year before Eid al-Fitr a great many people leave Jakarta and its neighbouring cities for their home towns. In 2019 Eid fell on Wednesday 5 June. If a city empties, does it dim? This page looks for the exodus in six weeks of nightly light. It does not show where it was expected, in the cities. On the roads, one night stands out: the Sunday of the return.

What this page can and cannot say. The cities give a negative result: a rhythm of the satellite's own is large enough to hide whatever Eid does to Jakarta's light. On the roads one night stands out, the Sunday of the return (the last map on this page). None of it has been checked against traffic counts or any other record on the ground. The reference nights are 15 to 27 May, inside Ramadan, which has night-time habits of its own.

1 · What the satellite adds of its own

A city that seems to breathe

Measured night by night, the light of Jakarta, Bekasi, Depok and Tangerang rises and falls by about . Much of it follows the viewing angle, not the city. The swings come back every five to six nights, and again at eleven and at sixteen: two nights eleven apart resemble each other closely (correlation ), two nights eight apart are opposites (). That is the rhythm of the satellite. Its viewing angle repeats every sixteen nights and swings from overhead to the side and back three times in between: over Jakarta in these six weeks it ran from to degrees from overhead (hover a point). A city looked at straight down and from the side is not the same brightness.

2 · The question

The exodus that barely shows

The fair comparison is between nights seen from the same angle, sixteen nights apart. Set each Eid night against its partners and it comes out at to of them, across comparisons. Now do the same for pairs in which neither night is Eid: the later night comes out at to of the earlier, median , across . The Eid nights sit inside that spread. A second way uses more nights: measure every cell only against reference nights seen from within degrees of the same angle. The Eid nights then read ; the other nights run from to . Whatever Eid did to the light of Jakarta and its neighbours, this data cannot tell it from two ordinary nights.

3 · A warning from the data

A map that looks like a finding

Rank the districts by their light on Eid night and the result is almost too neat. Every city people leave sits at the bottom; every home district that was seen that night sits at the top. It looks like the exodus drawn in light. It is not evidence. A split as clean, with the home district the brighter in at least 95% of pairs, appears on of ordinary nights in these six weeks. Why was not measured here; the two groups lie at opposite ends of the map, far enough apart to be seen through different air and from a slightly different angle. A pattern that fits the story on the night of the story has to be checked on all the other nights too.

4 · Where it does show

One night stands out: the Sunday of the return

Stop asking how bright the cities are and ask instead which dark places lit up. Take every land cell of Java, lit or not, from Banten to Banyuwangi, and subtract its light on the reference nights. On the nights of the return a line appears from Jakarta east along the north coast as far as Semarang, with a branch towards Bandung. To measure it, the line was traced on those nights, between 107.3 and 110.6 E where it is a line: land cells that are near-dark on the reference nights (under 3 nW) and at least 1.5 nW brighter on 8 to 11 June. Cells chosen for having brightened will of course read brighter on those nights, and a dim cell's light also swings with the satellite's viewing angle. So the test that counts is a night against another night seen from the same angle, sixteen nights away, with the dark countryside around the line subtracted.

By that test, on Sunday 9 June the line was nW brighter than on 24 May and nW brighter than on 25 June, on cells whose usual light is about nW. Monday 10 June stood nW above 25 May. It is not an effect of the choosing either: cells picked on 10 and 11 June alone, nights that say nothing about the 9th, read nW above the countryside on 9 June. The nights of leaving show nothing of the kind. Against their same-angle nights in mid June, 29, 30 and 31 May read , and nW: no brighter. And the last nights before Eid, 1 to 4 June, were under cloud.

Every land cell is compared, not only the lit ones; the sea is left out, and boats and platforms with it: its mean light over the nights of the window it was seen, minus its mean over the reference nights, in nW per cm² per sr. A cell needs two nights in the window; of the land had them in the nights shown, and grey is not seen, which is not the same as dark. Sharp straight edges in the east are where the product marked a block of cells unusable, not something on the ground. The speckle over Jakarta and Bandung is the viewing rhythm shown in the first section, not traffic. On the leaving nights much of the countryside reads 0.5 to 1.5 nW brighter: that is mostly the viewing angle of those nights, not traffic. The nights of 1 to 4 June, the last before Eid, were almost wholly under cloud and cannot be shown.

What can be said, then, is narrow and firm: across the north of Java, dark cells along the corridor to Semarang were several times brighter than usual at about 01:30 on the morning of Monday 10 June, the end of the Sunday of the return, and nothing like it shows on the nights of leaving. What it was is a guess. Traffic is ours, but the light could as well be lamps at rest areas and toll gates, and the towns along the way were somewhat brighter that night too. The roads on the map do not settle it: dim cells that flicker lie along main roads and the edges of towns on any group of nights. What sets the return apart is how many there were: cells passed the test on the return nights, against in three ordinary windows of later June. The chart above is drawn against the reference nights, so its bars on the nights of leaving are mostly the viewing angle and the choosing of the cells; only the comparisons at one angle carry a claim. Where the line ends was decided by looking at the map. And the satellite sees who is on the road in the small hours, not the day's traffic.

5 · What it would take

What would settle the cities

For the cities the negative result stands, and it says where the limit is. Matching the viewing angle halves the scatter over Lombok and Bali; over Jakarta it removes much less, and the cities' light still moves by about between ordinary nights seen alike. Something other than the angle is at work there: the side of the swath, the air, or the city itself, and this page cannot say which. To go further would need a year of nights, a reference outside Ramadan, and a second year to tell a holiday from a coincidence. None of that is done here.
